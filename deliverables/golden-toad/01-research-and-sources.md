# Golden toad — Research and sources

Research date: 2026-07-29

## Research summary

- **Accepted name:** Golden toad, *Incilius periglenes*. The American Museum of Natural History places it in family Bufonidae and records the accepted combination *Incilius periglenes*.
- **Status:** Extinct. IUCN reports that extensive searches have found no animal since the last confirmed record in 1989.
- **Range:** The species was endemic to Costa Rica and known from a very small high-elevation area around Monteverde, roughly 1,500–1,620 metres above sea level.
- **Habitat:** Lower montane cloud and elfin forest. Its known habitat lay within protected forest and remained in relatively good condition when the species disappeared.
- **Appearance:** Small, compact true toads with extreme sex differences. Adult males were vivid orange. Larger females were dark and patterned with yellow and red.
- **Seasonal behaviour:** For most of the year the animals were difficult to find. They emerged in large numbers during a short rainy-season breeding period.
- **Breeding:** Golden toads used shallow temporary pools among forest roots. Their eggs and tadpoles depended on water lasting long enough to complete development.
- **Nineteen eighty-seven:** Researchers recorded more than 1,500 adults at five breeding pools. Drying pools caused extreme breeding failure; at most 29 tadpoles completed metamorphosis.
- **Collapse:** Surveys during the 1988–1990 breeding seasons found only 11 toads in total.
- **Last confirmed record:** IUCN identifies a single male found in 1989 as the last confirmed animal. Published scientific accounts place the record in May; the narration uses that cautious month-level wording and does not claim to know the animal's later fate.
- **Likely pressures:** The extinction cannot be assigned to one proven cause with certainty. The 1986–1987 El Niño event produced an exceptional dry interval. Chytridiomycosis, caused by the chytrid fungus, is also a leading explanation in a region where the disease drove severe amphibian losses.
- **Main lesson:** Habitat protection alone cannot block every threat. Narrow-range amphibians also need disease surveillance, stable breeding water, climate planning, and rapid action when counts collapse.

## Accuracy notes

- Do not say habitat destruction directly erased the golden toad. Its known forest was protected and remained largely intact.
- Do not present climate change, El Niño, or chytrid as the sole proven cause. State that drought and disease are leading, interacting explanations and that their exact balance remains debated.
- Do not confuse the Costa Rican golden toad with the living Kihansi spray toad, which is sometimes called a golden toad.
- Do not show every animal as orange. The bright orange individuals were adult males; females were larger and dark with yellow and red markings.
- Do not say 1,500 healthy young survived in 1987. More than 1,500 adults gathered, but at most 29 tadpoles completed metamorphosis.
- Do not imply that the final male was known to die on the day he was seen.
- Keep *Incilius periglenes* in metadata rather than spoken narration.
