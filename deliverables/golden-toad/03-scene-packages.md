## Intro — The Golden Toad

**Subtitle:** A two-year collapse
**Hook:** Fifteen hundred became one.
**Image:** `00-intro.png`

In nineteen eighty-seven, a hidden cloud forest seemed to turn gold. More than fifteen hundred tiny toads gathered beside rain-filled pools. Two years later, researchers found just one. It was the last golden toad ever confirmed. An entire species had vanished while its forest still stood.

## Scene 1 — A World Only Kilometres Wide

**Subtitle:** High in Costa Rica's cloud forest
**Image:** `01-cloud-forest.png`

The golden toad lived in one tiny part of Costa Rica. Its known home lay high in the cloud forest near Monteverde. Mist kept the leaves wet, while moss covered roots and fallen wood. The entire known range measured only a few square kilometres. A change in one small forest could touch the whole species.

**Fact note:** The species was known only from a small high-elevation area near Monteverde, Costa Rica.

## Scene 2 — Two Colours, One Species

**Subtitle:** Only the males were golden
**Image:** `02-two-colours.png`

The name told only half the story. Adult males were a brilliant orange, almost too bright for the dark forest floor. Females were larger and wore dark skin marked with yellow and red. Both had the compact body and short legs of a true toad. Together, they formed one of nature's most striking pairs.

**Fact note:** Males were vivid orange; larger females had dark colouring with yellow and red markings.

## Scene 3 — Hidden Beneath the Forest

**Subtitle:** Seen for only a few weeks
**Image:** `03-hidden.png`

For most of the year, the golden toad was almost invisible. It stayed hidden in damp spaces beneath roots and leaf litter. Its food was likely the small insects and other tiny animals found on the forest floor. Then the dry season began to break. The first hard rains called the toads into the open.

**Fact note:** Golden toads were seen mainly during their brief breeding season after the rains began.

## Scene 4 — When the Rain Arrived

**Subtitle:** A brief race to breed
**Image:** `04-breeding-rain.png`

Heavy rain created shallow pools between the tree roots. In a few days, males crowded around the water and waited for females. The gathering was loud, bright, and brief. Eggs and tadpoles had to develop before the pools disappeared. The species had built its future around a narrow window of rain.

**Fact note:** The species bred explosively in temporary rain pools during a short annual season.

## Scene 5 — Fifteen Hundred, Then Failure

**Subtitle:** The pools dried in nineteen eighty-seven
**Image:** `05-dry-pools.png`

In the spring of nineteen eighty-seven, researchers counted more than fifteen hundred adult golden toads at five pools. It looked like abundance. But the rain stopped, and the shallow water shrank. Thousands of eggs and tadpoles were lost as the pools dried. At most, only twenty-nine young toads completed the change to life on land.

**Fact note:** More than 1,500 adults were recorded in 1987, but at most 29 tadpoles completed metamorphosis.

## Scene 6 — The Collapse

**Subtitle:** Almost nothing returned
**Image:** `06-collapse.png`

Researchers came back when the next breeding season began. The forest was still there, and the pools filled again. The golden crowds did not return. Across repeated searches from nineteen eighty-eight through nineteen ninety, only eleven toads were found. A population once counted by the thousand had collapsed almost overnight.

**Fact note:** Only 11 golden toads were found during breeding-habitat surveys from 1988 through 1990.

## Scene 7 — The Last Golden Toad

**Subtitle:** One male in nineteen eighty-nine
**Image:** `07-last-male.png`

In May of nineteen eighty-nine, a single male appeared. He was small, bright orange, and alone. No one knew that this ordinary field record would become the final one. Later searches covered the known range, but no golden toad answered the rain. That male remains the last confirmed member of his species.

**Fact note:** A single male seen in May 1989 is the species' last confirmed record.

## Scene 8 — A Forest Without Its Toad

**Subtitle:** Protection could not explain the silence
**Image:** `08-empty-forest.png`

This was not a simple story of a forest being cut down. The golden toad lived inside protected cloud forest, and much of that habitat remained. Yet a reserve could not control the weather, stop a hidden disease, or protect a species beyond its boundary. The empty pools became a scientific mystery. The answer had to reach beyond habitat loss alone.

**Fact note:** The species disappeared despite its known range lying within protected forest.

## Scene 9 — Drought, Disease, and Uncertainty

**Subtitle:** No single cause tells the whole story
**Image:** `09-causes.png`

The final collapse followed an exceptionally dry period linked to a powerful El Niño event. Dry pools explain the failed breeding season, but not every missing adult. Scientists also point to chytrid, a deadly fungus that swept through many American amphibians. Climate can change the conditions in which disease spreads and animals survive. The exact balance is still debated, so one cause should not carry the whole blame.

**Fact note:** An exceptional dry interval and chytridiomycosis are leading explanations; their relative roles remain debated.

## Scene 10 — A Warning From the Clouds

**Subtitle:** Small ranges leave little room
**Image:** `10-legacy.png`

The golden toad became an early symbol of the worldwide amphibian crisis. Its tiny range made every bad season more dangerous. Its disappearance also showed that a protected map is only the beginning of protection. Conservation must watch disease, climate, water, and sudden changes before numbers crash. The warning from Monteverde still belongs to living species everywhere.

**Fact note:** The golden toad is an emblem of global amphibian decline and the risks faced by narrow-range species.

## Outro — Protection Must See the Invisible

**Subtitle:** A standing forest can still lose a species
**Hook:** Its forest survived. The toad did not.
**Image:** Reuse `10-legacy.png`

The golden toad left no final photograph of a rescue and no captive group to return. It left an empty pool and a lesson about threats we cannot always see. Living amphibians still need faster disease checks, stable wetlands, and careful climate planning. Next, we cross the grasslands that once darkened beneath swarms of Rocky Mountain locusts.
