# Passenger pigeon scene packages

All viewer-facing copy is English. The narration uses short sentences and familiar words for clear TTS. The scientific name remains in metadata rather than spoken narration.

## Intro — `00-intro.png`

- Title: The Passenger Pigeon
- Subtitle: *Ectopistes migratorius*
- Hook: Billions of birds. Then silence.
- TTS: “Imagine looking up and seeing no end to the birds above you. Their wings turn daylight into shadow. The sound rolls across the land like a storm. Passenger pigeons once travelled in numbers that seemed impossible to destroy. Less than a century later, every one of them was gone.”
- Visual: Low-angle cold open from a dark forest floor as a vast living river of pigeons blocks the morning sky.

## 01 — `01-opening.png`

- Title / subtitle: Built to Travel / A pigeon made for distance
- TTS: “A passenger pigeon was larger and more streamlined than a mourning dove. It had long, pointed wings and a long, narrow tail. The male carried a blue-grey back, a warm rose chest and a shining neck. The female was browner and softer in colour. Both were built to travel far and fast.”
- Fact note: Males and females had different plumage; males were brighter, while females were browner and paler.
- Motion / focus: zoom-to-focus; X 0.65, Y 0.48. Title 1.0–4.2s; subtitle 4.6–7.6s.
- Visual: Close side portrait of a male and female on separate branches, showing the plumage difference.

## 02 — `02-flocks.png`

- Title / subtitle: Strength in Numbers / A moving wall of wings
- TTS: “The species survived through numbers. Huge flocks could overwhelm hawks and other predators. They also helped the birds search a continent for food. Historical witnesses described flights that darkened the sky for hours. The total population was never counted. It is often estimated at three to five billion birds.”
- Fact note: The billions figure is a historical estimate based on eyewitness accounts, not a modern census.
- Motion / focus: slow-zoom-out; X 0.48, Y 0.35. Title 1.0–4.2s; subtitle 4.6–7.6s.
- Visual: Extreme aerial view of a dense flock bending like a river over autumn forest and farmland.

## 03 — `03-habitat.png`

- Title / subtitle: Following the Forest / Acorns, beechnuts and chestnuts
- TTS: “Passenger pigeons did not follow one simple route. They moved wherever eastern forests produced the most food. Acorns, beechnuts and chestnuts were vital. Seeds, berries and small insects added more. A rich crop could draw a flock from far away. Their world depended on vast connected forests and the freedom to keep moving.”
- Fact note: The birds were highly mobile and tracked unpredictable crops of tree nuts across eastern North America.
- Motion / focus: pan-left-to-right; X 0.34, Y 0.58. Title 1.0–4.2s; subtitle 4.6–7.6s.
- Visual: Shoreline-level forest scene with birds dropping through red-oak and beech branches to feed among fallen nuts.

## 04 — `04-nesting.png`

- Title / subtitle: Cities in the Trees / Breeding by the millions
- TTS: “When the birds nested, they gathered in enormous colonies. Nests filled tree after tree across many kilometres. The forest became loud, crowded and alive. Large groups offered safety from natural predators. But they also placed adults, eggs and young in one visible place. Once people found a colony, the birds had nowhere to hide.”
- Fact note: Historical nesting colonies extended across large areas of hardwood forest and could contain millions of birds.
- Motion / focus: pan-bottom-to-top; X 0.55, Y 0.43. Title 1.0–4.2s; subtitle 4.6–7.6s.
- Visual: Upward view through a nesting colony, with many occupied stick nests layered into the canopy.

## 05 — `05-ecosystem.png`

- Title / subtitle: A Force of Nature / When a flock changed the forest
- TTS: “A flock this large changed every place it entered. The birds consumed huge crops of seeds. Their weight broke branches. Their droppings altered the ground below crowded roosts. They fed predators and competed with other animals for food. The passenger pigeon was not simply living in the forest. In great numbers, it helped reshape it.”
- Fact note: The scale of feeding and roosting affected vegetation, tree limbs, predators and other seed-eating animals.
- Motion / focus: gentle-diagonal; X 0.72, Y 0.42. Title 1.0–4.2s; subtitle 4.6–7.6s.
- Visual: Layered wide view of a crowded roost at dusk, broken branches and forest animals below without showing injury.

## 06 — `06-market-hunting.png`

- Title / subtitle: Hunting Becomes an Industry / Railways, markets and mass killing
- TTS: “People had hunted passenger pigeons for generations. In the nineteenth century, the scale changed. Telegraph messages could reveal where a flock had settled. Railways carried hunters in and crates of birds out. Nesting colonies became market sites. Adults were shot or trapped. Young birds were taken from nests. Abundance had become a commercial target.”
- Fact note: Telegraph and railway networks helped market hunters locate colonies and ship large numbers of birds to cities.
- Motion / focus: pan-right-to-left; X 0.28, Y 0.57. Title 1.0–4.2s; subtitle 4.6–7.6s.
- Visual: Non-graphic historical rail siding with crates and a distant flock; hunters remain small and no dead birds are shown.

## 07 — `07-decline.png`

- Title / subtitle: When the Flocks Broke / Hunting, disturbance and forest loss
- TTS: “By the late nineteenth century, the great flocks were breaking apart. Commercial hunting continued. Nesting attempts were disturbed again and again. Hardwood forests were also being cleared and divided. The birds' social system may have made recovery harder. A small flock could not find food or breed as effectively as a vast one.”
- Fact note: Hunting and habitat change acted together; dependence on large groups probably added vulnerability, but no exact minimum flock size is known.
- Motion / focus: slow-zoom-out; X 0.25, Y 0.62. Title 1.0–4.2s; subtitle 4.6–7.6s.
- Visual: High winter landscape showing a tiny scattered flock over a patchwork of forest stumps and surviving woodland.

## 08 — `08-last-wild.png`

- Title / subtitle: The Wild Sky Empties / Last accepted records: around 1900
- TTS: “In only a few decades, abundance became absence. By the 1890s, sightings were rare. The last widely accepted wild records came around 1900. Searches and rewards failed to find a surviving nest or colony. There was no single moment when everyone knew the wild population was gone. The sky simply stopped bringing them back.”
- Fact note: The Smithsonian documents an authenticated wild capture in Pike County, Ohio, on 24 March 1900; later reports did not reveal a population.
- Motion / focus: pan-top-to-bottom; X 0.72, Y 0.34. Title 1.0–4.2s; subtitle 4.6–7.6s.
- Visual: A lone female crossing a huge empty evening sky above bare hardwood branches, with no hunting shown.

## 09 — `09-martha.png`

- Title / subtitle: Martha / The last known passenger pigeon
- TTS: “A few passenger pigeons remained in captivity. Breeding attempts failed as their numbers fell. The last known bird was a female named Martha. She lived alone at Cincinnati Zoo. On 1 September 1914, Martha died. Her body was sent to the Smithsonian. A species once measured in billions had ended with one bird.”
- Fact note: Martha died at Cincinnati Zoo on 1 September 1914 and is preserved by the Smithsonian.
- Motion / focus: zoom-to-focus; X 0.63, Y 0.49. Title 1.0–4.2s; subtitle 4.6–7.6s.
- Visual: Respectful historical zoo portrait of Martha alone on a branch, with soft enclosure bars far out of focus.

## 10 — `10-legacy.png`

- Title / subtitle: Abundance Is Not Safety / A lesson that changed conservation
- TTS: “The passenger pigeon destroyed a dangerous belief. No species is too numerous to fail. Its extinction helped turn public attention toward stronger wildlife protection. The lesson still matters. Counting animals is not enough. We must also protect breeding places, food, movement and social systems before a rapid decline becomes permanent.”
- Fact note: The extinction became a major symbol for the early twentieth-century conservation movement.
- Motion / focus: slow-zoom-in; X 0.38, Y 0.52. Title 1.0–4.2s; subtitle 4.6–7.6s.
- Visual: Museum specimen in the foreground with a bright living wetland and modern migratory birds beyond, symbolising protection without implying revival.
