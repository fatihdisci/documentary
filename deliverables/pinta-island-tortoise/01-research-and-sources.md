# Pinta Island tortoise — Research and sources

Research date: 2026-07-27

## Research summary

- **Accepted name used in this package:** Pinta Island tortoise, *Chelonoidis abingdonii*. The Charles Darwin Foundation treats this as an accepted species. Some current taxonomic systems instead place it within the Galápagos giant tortoise complex as *Chelonoidis niger abingdonii*. The video avoids turning that classification difference into a false controversy.
- **Range:** Endemic to Pinta, a small, dry volcanic island in the northern Galápagos archipelago of Ecuador.
- **Appearance:** A giant saddleback tortoise with a high-fronted shell opening, long neck and long legs. This body form helped it reach sparse shrubs and cactus growth in an arid environment.
- **Ecology:** Like other Galápagos giant tortoises, it was a large herbivore and ecosystem engineer. Browsing, trampling, trail-making, nutrient movement and seed dispersal helped shape island vegetation.
- **Historical decline:** Whalers, fur sealers and other visitors heavily exploited Pinta tortoises during the nineteenth century. This pressure had already reduced the population to near zero before the twentieth century.
- **Introduced goats:** Fishermen released three goats on Pinta in 1959. Galápagos Conservancy reports that the population was estimated at roughly 40,000 by 1970, with severe damage to vegetation and remaining tortoise habitat.
- **Last known pure individual:** A Hungarian scientist, József Vágvölgyi, found the male later called Lonesome George on 1 December 1971. Park staff moved him to the tortoise center on Santa Cruz for protection in 1972. The Charles Darwin Foundation database gives 1973 as the start of his residence at the breeding center, so the narration simply says he was moved the following year.
- **Breeding attempts:** George lived with females from Wolf Volcano and later with Española tortoises. Eggs were produced, but no offspring survived; the documented 2008 clutch was sterile.
- **Extinction:** Lonesome George was found dead on 24 June 2012. A necropsy found signs of aging and concluded that he died of natural causes. His exact age was unknown, though he was estimated to be more than one hundred years old. His death marked the extinction of the last known pure Pinta Island tortoise.
- **Genetic legacy:** Genetic work identified living tortoises on Wolf Volcano with partial Pinta ancestry. A peer-reviewed study reported 17 such hybrids, including possible first-generation hybrids. A later Galápagos National Park expedition recovered a young female with a strong Pinta genetic component. These discoveries preserve part of the lineage, but they do not prove that a pure Pinta tortoise survives.
- **Island restoration:** Pinta was declared goat-free in 2003. In 2010, 39 sterilized hybrid tortoises were released as ecological replacements to help restore ecosystem processes while longer-term recovery options are studied.

## Accuracy notes

- Do not say Lonesome George was the last Galápagos tortoise. He was the last known pure member of the Pinta lineage.
- Do not say goats were the only cause of extinction. Heavy historical collection had already brought the population close to collapse.
- Do not present George's age as exact.
- Do not describe the Wolf Volcano hybrids as pure Pinta tortoises or direct offspring of George.
- The spelling *abingdoni* appears in older institutional and scientific material. This package uses the accepted spelling *abingdonii* while preserving source titles as published.
