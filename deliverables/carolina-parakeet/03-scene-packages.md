## Full TTS copy

**Intro:** A flash of green once moved through the woods of the eastern United States. It came with sharp calls, long tails and bright yellow heads. This was the Carolina parakeet. It was the only parrot native to this part of America. Then the calls faded. Before anyone truly understood its life, the bird was gone.

**01:** The Carolina parakeet was not a bird from a far away rain forest. It lived across much of the eastern and central United States. Its green body blended into leaves. Its yellow head and warm red face stood out in the sun. A long tail trailed behind it in flight. To people who saw a flock, it must have looked like a piece of the tropics moving through home woods.

**02:** The birds used many kinds of places. River woods, wet ground, open woodland and the edge of farm land all appear in old reports. They were most at home where large trees still stood. Their old range was broad, but it was not one simple block. Groups in different places may have lived in different ways. That makes the story harder to read today, but it also shows how much ground the bird once covered.

**03:** Carolina parakeets were social birds. They travelled in groups and made loud calls as they moved. Historical accounts describe them as restless and close together. A flock could help birds find food and warn one another of danger. But close ties could also bring risk. When one bird was hurt, others were said to return and circle nearby. That detail appears in old accounts, and it helps explain why people could take many birds from one place.

**04:** The parakeet ate a wide range of food. Seeds, fruit, nuts and farm crops all appear in the record. This made the bird flexible. It could feed in woods, along rivers and near fields. But it also brought the bird into conflict with people. A flock in a crop field was seen as a loss. Some farmers shot the birds. Others later noticed that the birds ate troublesome weeds. By then, the chance to protect them was already slipping away.

**05:** Old trees gave the birds places to rest and nest. A hollow trunk could hold shelter from rain and wind. Across the country, large woods were cut for farms, towns and roads. Wet places were drained. The loss did not happen in one day. It spread year by year. A bird that could use many kinds of land still needed safe old trees. As those trees fell, the places where a flock could settle became fewer.

**06:** The Carolina parakeet was hard to ignore. Its calls were loud. Its colors were bright. Farmers could see it feeding near crops. Hunters shot birds for sport and for food. Some were caught for cages. Their feathers were used in fashion. None of these pressures tells the whole story alone. Together, they cut into a bird already losing its home. What once made the parakeet easy to love also made it easy to find.

**07:** By the late nineteenth century, reports of the birds were becoming less common. The cause of the final drop is still not clear. Disease has been suggested. So has the loss of nest sites. The best records do show a world changing fast around them. Forests were broken apart. People killed birds in many places. The film does not need one neat answer. It is enough to see that a bright and common bird was not kept safe.

**08:** The last years in the wild are hard to pin down. Some reports came from the south after the bird had become rare. A few may have been real. Others cannot be checked now. Recent work suggests that groups in the east and west may have vanished at different times. That is an important clue, not a final answer. What is certain is that the loud flocks did not return. The land had grown quiet without them.

**09:** The last known Carolina parakeet lived in a zoo. His name was Incas. On 21 February 1918, he died at Cincinnati Zoo. That date marks the end of the species we know for certain. There were stories of birds still living in the wild, but no group was found again. A parrot that had filled woods and river land was now known only from old notes, old skins and a few faded images.

**10:** The Carolina parakeet is gone, but its story is not only about the past. It shows why a familiar animal can be missed until it is too late. A species can live across a wide area and still lose the places and peace it needs. The lesson is simple. Keep wild homes whole. Protect animals before they become rare. And listen for the voices that are still here.

**Outro:** The Carolina parakeet vanished before its life was fully understood. We cannot bring back its call. But we can protect the places and species that still have one. Next time, we meet the Pinta Island tortoise. Subscribe for the next story.
